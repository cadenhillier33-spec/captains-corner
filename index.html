Week of March 23 — 27, 2026

01

The Horizon Scan

Macro Intelligence & Institutional Flow

What's happening: The S&P 500 broke below its 200-day moving average for the first time in 214 sessions last week, marking a decisive technical breakdown as geopolitical and monetary headwinds converged. The index fell 1.6% to close at 6,506 on Friday, the Dow shed 2.0% to 45,577, and the Nasdaq dropped 2.0% to 21,648. The catalyst was a one-two punch: the Fed held rates at 3.50-3.75% on Wednesday with a dot plot showing the committee deeply split—seven members projecting no cuts in 2026, seven projecting one cut—while the Iran conflict escalated dramatically on Friday when Iraq declared force majeure on all foreign-operated oilfields and Iranian drones struck two Kuwaiti refineries. Brent crude surged 3.3% to $112.19/bbl on Friday alone, with WTI settling at $98.32.

What it means: The FOMC meeting delivered exactly what bears feared: a committee with no urgency to cut rates despite slowing growth. The updated Summary of Economic Projections raised the 2026 inflation forecast to 2.7% (core PCE) while trimming GDP growth to 2.4%. The dot plot was the headline—a near-even split between zero cuts and one cut this year signals the Fed is paralyzed between the growth scare and energy-driven inflation. The 10-year Treasury yield jumped 11 basis points to 4.391% on the week, reflecting the market's acceptance that "higher for longer" is now baseline. FedEx was the lone bright spot: earnings of $5.25/share crushed estimates of $4.14, revenue hit $24.0B vs. $23.5B expected, and the company raised full-year guidance. Shares rose 2.8% on Thursday. But the Friday oil shock overwhelmed everything—Iraq's southern port exports collapsed from 3.3M bpd to 900K bpd, and Saudi officials warned crude could hit $180 if disruptions persist through April.

Our view: The 200-day moving average break is significant—it's the market's line in the sand, and we're now below it. Three straight losing weeks for the S&P 500 is the worst streak in nearly a year. The VIX spiked 11.3% on Friday to 26.78, confirming fear is back in control. Gold pulled back slightly to $4,575 as profit-taking hit the precious metal, but the longer-term bid remains intact. Bitcoin slipped modestly to $70,193, consolidating after its recent surge. The key question now is whether 6,500 holds on the S&P—if it doesn't, 6,000 is the next major support. We're maintaining maximum defensive positioning: overweight energy, defense, and cash; underweight growth tech and consumer discretionary. Flash PMIs on Tuesday and revised consumer sentiment on Friday will test whether the real economy is bending under the weight of $112 oil.

02

Deep Water Analysis

The 200-Day Break: What the Technical Breakdown Means for Markets

Why this matters: On March 19, the S&P 500 closed at 6,606—decisively below its 200-day moving average of 6,615 for the first time since May 2025. This wasn't a single-day fluke: by Friday's close at 6,506, the index was nearly 100 points below that critical support line. The descent began from the record high of 6,978 in late January, as AI euphoria gave way to the harsh reality of a Middle East war, $112 oil, and a Fed that has no room to cut rates. The FOMC's March 18 decision confirmed this: rates held at 3.50-3.75%, with Fed Chair Powell's press conference offering no dovish pivot. The committee raised its 2026 inflation forecast to 2.7% while maintaining GDP growth at 2.4%—but the market is pricing in something worse. The Dow, S&P, and Nasdaq all broke below their respective 200-day averages last week, a synchronized breakdown that hasn't occurred since October 2023. Iraq's force majeure declaration on Friday—halting exports from 3.3M bpd to just 900K bpd at its southern Basra ports—was the week's final blow.

The bottom line: The market is now in a confirmed technical downtrend. Three straight losing weeks, a broken 200-day moving average, and a VIX that spiked 11.3% to 26.78 on Friday paint a picture of escalating fear. FedEx's blowout quarter ($5.25 EPS vs. $4.14 estimate, revenue of $24.0B vs. $23.5B, raised full-year guidance to $19.30-$20.10) proved that strong fundamentals still exist—but even FDX only managed a 2.8% gain before Friday's oil shock erased broader market optimism. The Fed's dot plot split—exactly seven members for zero cuts and seven for one cut—tells you the committee itself doesn't know what happens next. Stephen Miran's dissent (he wanted a 25bp cut) underscores the tension between growth concerns and inflation reality. With Brent at $112 and Saudi Arabia warning of $180 if disruptions persist through April, energy-driven inflation is the dominant risk. UBS maintains its year-end S&P target of 7,700, implying a 14% rally from here—but that requires a resolution to the conflict that currently shows no signs of materializing.

The Contrarian Compass

"The 200-day moving average break has everyone panicking—and that's exactly when you should be taking notes, not taking exits. Yes, the S&P 500 broke below its 200-DMA for the first time in 214 sessions. Yes, Iraq just pulled 2.4 million barrels per day off the market. Yes, the Fed is paralyzed with a 7-7 dot plot split. But here's the contrarian read: FedEx just delivered earnings 27% above consensus, raised guidance, and is spinning off its freight division—a company deeply tied to the real economy just told you business is better than Wall Street thinks. Meanwhile, UBS is holding its 7,700 year-end target, implying a 14% upside from Friday's close. The market is pricing in worst-case oil at $180, a frozen Fed, and a recession. If even one of those assumptions proves wrong—a ceasefire, a surprise cut, or simply $112 oil not going higher—the snapback will be violent. We're not buying yet. But the shopping list is getting longer by the day."

Three Ways This Week Could Play Out

The FOMC is behind us, but the aftermath is still unfolding. This week's data will test whether the economy can absorb $112 oil and a frozen Fed. Tuesday brings Flash Manufacturing and Services PMI readings—the first real-time gauge of business conditions since the Iraq force majeure. Thursday delivers weekly jobless claims, which will be watched for any signs of labor market stress from energy costs. Friday's revised University of Michigan Consumer Sentiment (expected 55.5) will tell us if consumers are cracking under $3.70+ gas prices. Multiple Fed speakers throughout the week will parse the post-FOMC message.

Scenario A: Data Resilience
"Flash PMIs hold above 50, jobless claims stay subdued, consumer sentiment stabilizes—economy bends but doesn't break"
Likely market reaction:
• S&P 500 bounces off 6,500 support, reclaims 6,600
• VIX eases back toward 22-24 range
• Oversold tech names see a relief rally, especially after FedEx's strong signal

Our take: This would be the "it's bad but not catastrophic" scenario. Resilient PMIs would suggest the economy can absorb high oil prices in the near term. We'd look to selectively add quality names but maintain defensive anchors. The 200-DMA needs to be reclaimed before we turn constructive.
Scenario B: Cracks Emerge
"Manufacturing PMI drops below 50, consumer sentiment plunges below 50, jobless claims spike—stagflation fears accelerate"
Likely market reaction:
• S&P 500 breaks 6,500 and tests 6,300 support
• Treasury yields paradoxically drop as recession fears outweigh inflation
• VIX surges past 30, triggering systematic selling cascades

Our take: This is the scenario we're positioning for. If $112 oil is already showing up in PMI data within days of the Iraq force majeure, it confirms the "stagflation is here" thesis. We stay maximum defensive: energy, defense, cash, and short-duration Treasuries. No dip-buying until we see capitulation.
Scenario C: Geopolitical De-escalation
"Diplomatic progress on Iran—Hormuz shipping lanes reopen, oil drops below $90, Iraq lifts force majeure"
Likely market reaction:
• S&P 500 surges 4-6% in a massive relief rally above the 200-DMA
• Oil crashes 20%+, unwinding the entire war premium
• Airlines, consumer discretionary, and travel names explode higher

Our take: Still low probability, but the payoff is enormous. A ceasefire would remove the inflation overhang and the growth headwind simultaneously. We'd aggressively rotate into oversold consumer and travel names while trimming energy. This would be the buying opportunity of the year—but we're not betting on it.

We're positioned for Scenario B (cracks emerge). Flash PMIs on Tuesday are the week's biggest catalyst—they'll be the first hard data reflecting $112 oil. Friday's UMich consumer sentiment revision will confirm or deny the consumer stress thesis. Fed speakers throughout the week will elaborate on the split dot plot. Cash remains king.

Stocks We're Watching

Not Recommendations

Three names capturing our attention after the FOMC and the oil shock: a shipping giant that just proved the real economy is stronger than the market thinks, the energy sector that's been our top call for weeks and just got another catalyst, and a beaten-down consumer discretionary play that could explode higher if geopolitical tensions ease.

#1
FDX
FedEx Corporation
📊 Earnings Beat

FedEx delivered one of the strongest earnings reports of the season: $5.25 EPS vs. $4.14 estimates (27% beat), revenue of $24.0B vs. $23.5B expected, and raised full-year guidance to $19.30-$20.10. The "Network 2.0" transformation is exceeding its $1B cost savings target, and the June 1 freight spinoff approaches. Shares rose 2.8% on Thursday before Friday's broad market sell-off. The signal here is powerful: a company deeply tied to global trade volumes just told you the real economy is performing better than the market is pricing in. If the macro picture stabilizes even slightly, FDX has significant upside from current levels.

#2
XLE
Energy Select Sector SPDR
🐋 War Premium Accelerating

Energy has been our top sector call for weeks, and the thesis just got turbocharged. Iraq's force majeure declaration pulled 2.4M bpd off the global market, Iranian drones struck two Kuwaiti refineries, and Brent closed at $112.19—up 25%+ since the war began. Saudi Arabia is warning of $180 oil if disruptions last through April. The energy sector is now the clearest beneficiary of the geopolitical regime: higher oil prices flow directly to revenue, and the sector is one of the few places offering both earnings growth and inflation protection simultaneously. We've been overweight energy since the conflict began and see no reason to trim.

#3
DAL
Delta Air Lines
🏛️ Maximum Pessimism Play

Airlines have been the single most punished sector since the Iran conflict began, with Delta down significantly alongside United and American on surging jet fuel costs. At current levels, the market is pricing in a prolonged period of $100+ oil with no relief. But here's the contrarian setup: if there's ANY diplomatic progress—even a partial reopening of the Strait of Hormuz—airlines would be the first and biggest beneficiary. Jet fuel is their largest variable cost, and the operational leverage works both ways. We're not buying yet—this is a "ceasefire bet" that requires a catalyst. But it sits on our shortlist as the highest-beta recovery trade in the market.
